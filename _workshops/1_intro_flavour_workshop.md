### Why does the same cup never taste quite the same to two people?

Taste feels like the most immediate of the senses - a liquid touches the tongue, and within moments we register bitter, sweet, sour, and decide whether we like it. But this apparent immediacy is misleading. What we call "flavour" is not detected so much as constructed: the brain fuses signals from the tongue with retronasal smell, temperature, texture, and even sound, and interprets the result through a lattice of memory, language, and expectation built over a lifetime of tasting. Two people holding the same cup are, in a meaningful sense, not tasting the same thing at all. One reason is biological - genetic variation in taste receptors means that a compound as simple as PROP can taste intensely bitter to one person and almost tasteless to another, a difference some researchers use to sort tasters into "supertaster," "taster," and "non-taster" populations. But biology is only the beginning of the story.

The more striking source of variation is cognitive. Studies in sensory and cognitive science have repeatedly shown that flavour perception is shaped by information that has nothing to do with the liquid itself: the colour of the vessel it is served in, the ambient sound of the room, the price on a label, or simply what we are told to expect before the first sip. These are not minor biases at the margins of taste - they are evidence that perception is an act of inference, not reception. The brain is not passively reporting what is on the tongue; it is actively predicting and constructing an experience, using every available cue to do so. This raises a question that reaches well beyond the tasting table: if something as seemingly direct as taste can be this malleable, what does that tell us about the reliability of perception more broadly - in how we see, hear, and remember the world?

This is the question the session sits with. Rather than delivering a fixed set of facts about coffee or wine, it invites participants to notice their own perceptual assumptions in real time - to taste something familiar and discover, often for the first time, how much of that experience they were quietly constructing themselves. A short guided tasting closes the session, turning the ideas discussed into something felt rather than only understood.
Around two hours. No prior background required.
